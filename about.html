About Valence / Saathvik Pramod

One question
led to another.

I’m Saathvik, a high-school student studying economics and building things with code. Valence grew out of the connections between the two.

An idea from economics

I started with
incentives.

Studying economics made me interested in how rules influence decisions. A change in a price, a subsidy, or a reward can make people respond differently, even when their underlying goals stay the same. I wanted to understand how those responses emerge from the details of a system.

During my logistics internship with Kochi Holdings, that interest became more practical. I worked on market-entry comparisons involving customs arrangements, duty deferral, and working capital. Evaluating the alternatives meant looking closely at how each arrangement worked and when its costs would arise.

That gave me a concrete way to think about something I had encountered in class. To understand a decision, I needed to understand the terms under which someone was making it.

Turning questions into models

I wanted to
change the inputs.

I carried that interest into my own coding projects. With Ceteris, I built a tool for constructing and exploring economic diagrams. It let me turn relationships I was studying into something people could work with themselves.

Causal Playground took a related question further. I could set up an underlying model, generate data, and examine how different statistical methods produced different estimates. Building it made the assumptions behind a result much harder to ignore.

I began thinking about other economic questions I could explore this way. If a small change in an assumption could change an estimate, what might a small change in a commercial rule do to the choices people made?

The question became more specific

Then I thought about
who writes the rules.

My research into consumer algorithms and contract complexity brought me back to the relationship between a decision and its terms. I was exploring how better computational tools might change the incentives of the businesses whose offers those tools evaluate.

From there, I started thinking about compensation. A company writes a commission plan to encourage sales. A salesperson makes decisions within that plan. But the company’s objective and the salesperson’s reward are different quantities.

A bonus threshold offered a specific question to model. Could offering a discount to close a deal before quarter-end increase the salesperson’s pay while reducing the company’s contribution from the sale?

What would the plan reward
someone for doing?
What would that choice cost?

Building Valence

That became
the first simulation.

Valence is my attempt to make that question inspectable. The prototype compares deal timing, discounts, and bonus structures, then shows how the same choice affects expected salesperson pay and company contribution.

It brings together the things I’ve been working on: using economics to frame a question, code to explore it, and explicit assumptions to explain the answer. I’m building it as a high-school student, with a focused model that I can keep testing and improving.

The question I started with has become more precise. When we put a reward in front of someone, what decision are we actually making worthwhile?
